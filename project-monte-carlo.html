Monte Carlo Simulation of Emergency Response for City Planning

September 2024 · 8 min read

Background

Monte Carlo Simulation is a statistical technique used to model and analyze complex systems or processes that involve randomness and uncertainty. It relies on generating a large number of random samples to simulate potential outcomes of a system, allowing for the estimation of the likelihood of different results.

This approach is particularly useful for decision-making under uncertainty, as it helps quantify risks, predict outcomes, and optimize processes across various fields such as finance, engineering, project management, and operations research. By running multiple iterations, Monte Carlo Simulation provides a range of possible results and their probabilities, helping to understand the most likely outcomes and assess the impact of variability in a system.

Introduction

This project is a Monte-Carlo simulation of a city with random emergency situations of different intensities that need to be attended to. The simulation takes place within a custom city with defined dimensions. The city can be configured to have emergency response units at different locations within the city. The objective of this simulation is to understand the optimal pattern for positioning and distribution of defined types of emergency response unit buildings such that the average response time to emergencies can be minimized.

Given a configuration with locations of the emergency response unit buildings, a simulation is run randomizing the time, location, and intensity of each emergency, along with possible traffic along the route. The solution finds the average emergency response time and success percentage of resolving the emergencies for the given configuration, and can be run over multiple configurations to determine which one produces the minimum response time and maximum success percentage for the emergencies. This type of simulation can be useful for city planning.

Simulation Environment Design

1. City Configuration

Assumptions:

  • A city is constructed using a set of coordinates where each coordinate represents the area around its vicinity, not a single point in the city.
  • Each city is divided into zones with equal areas, with a default of 9 coordinates per zone.
  • Every zone can have a different population which will be uniformly distributed within a zone.
  • There is a path between all horizontally & vertically adjacent coordinates with a default commute time of 3 minutes.

Configurable Parameters: Height of the city (in terms of zones), Width of the city (in terms of zones), Population of each zone.

Each zone in a city represents a 3×3 square grid. The diagram below shows an example city configured with a width and height of 4 zones.

The city has been imagined as a networkx graph, with a node representing a coordinate in the city that constitutes a general area, an edge representing the roads between two areas, and the edge attribute representing the time required by emergency response vehicles to traverse that road.

2. Emergency Units

Assumptions:

  • Emergencies are resolved using available teams from one or more emergency unit buildings.
  • Three types of emergency response unit buildings are defined: Large (7 teams), Medium (5 teams), and Small (3 teams).
  • Teams are unavailable when responding to an emergency — commuting to the location, resolving the emergency, and commuting back to base.

Configurable Parameters: Number of Small, Medium and Large Emergency Units; Position of each emergency unit as city coordinates.

3. Emergency Configuration

Assumptions:

  • Default Base Emergency Rate for a city is set to be 13.1615 emergencies per minute for a population of 200K, scaled according to the population of the configured city and zones.
  • Emergencies and Emergency Response units are both located on coordinates. Given that a coordinate represents an area and not a single point within the city, the commute time in such cases is assumed to be a minimal value of 1 minute.
  • Emergencies can be of 5 intensities with the below resolution time and personnel requirements.

Configurable Parameters: Probability of occurrence of each emergency intensity.

Randomized Variables

1. Time of Emergency

Randomizing the occurrence of emergencies with respect to time — the probability of an emergency occurring within the next minute in each zone is obtained using the Poisson distribution modeled using a real dataset.

For our simulation, we used a 911 emergency calls dataset from Montgomery County PD, which had date and time level records of emergency-related calls. The emergency rate was calculated as the number of emergencies occurring every minute within Montgomery County (population ~200K), which came out to be 13.16 — the base emergency rate. The base rate was then multiplied by the ratio of zone population to 200K to obtain the emergency rate for each zone.

2. Location of Emergency

Randomizing the location coordinates of the emergency within a zone using a uniform distribution, as the population is assumed to be uniformly distributed within each zone. These coordinates are x,y coordinates on an n×n grid which forms the city.

3. Intensity of Emergency

Each emergency can have an intensity on a scale of 1 to 5, with 1 being the lowest and 5 the highest. The number of emergency teams and the time taken to resolve the emergency are a deterministic function of intensity. Probabilities of each intensity are taken as user input, as intensity of emergencies occurring in a city are highly dependent on the city itself.

4. Traffic Delay

A baseline time of 3 minutes is considered as the time required to travel between any two adjacent nodes. A PERT probability distribution is used to generate the percent increase penalty value beyond the baseline time to account for traffic.

PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) estimates the expected delay by considering the most likely, best-case (optimistic), and worst-case (pessimistic) traffic conditions.

For this use-case, a low estimate of 0% increase and high estimate of 100% increase was considered. The average estimate was determined as the population of the zone as a percentage of the city's population. The random percent increase value is multiplied by a deterministic time scaling factor to account for variations in traffic at 4 different time periods of a day.

Conclusion

The Monte Carlo Simulation is a versatile and powerful analytical tool that can be applied to a wide range of scenarios beyond emergency response, such as financial forecasting, risk management, and supply chain optimization. By randomizing unpredictable variables, it provides realistic estimates of outcomes across different fields, helping to identify the most efficient and optimal strategies. Its ability to simulate numerous scenarios allows for better decision-making, making it an invaluable resource for addressing complex problems where uncertainty and variability are key factors.
